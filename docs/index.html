Why the model matters

Same data, different tails

All four models agree on ordinary years and disagree completely on rare ones. Only the Generalised Extreme Value (GEV) distribution, the model extreme-value theory prescribes for annual maxima, leaves room for 944 mm.

What the tests say

Findings

Distributions

GEV fits annual maxima best (AIC 522.8 vs 532.8 for Gamma). Detrended monsoon totals are Normal (Shapiro p = 0.57). Heavy-day counts are Poisson.

Hypothesis tests

Monsoons in 2010–24 averaged 3,077 mm, well above every earlier era (ANOVA F = 10.4, p < 0.001). The mean annual maximum did not change significantly (Welch t, p = 0.48).

Regression

Monsoon totals rise by about 101 mm per decade (p = 0.005; p = 0.054 after correcting for autocorrelation). Holding the season total fixed, each extra wet day lowers the worst day by 2.8 mm.

Validation

Trained on 1950–1990 and tested on 1991–2024, GEV exceedances stayed within expectation (4 vs 2.9 at the 10-year level, p = 0.33). The Poisson count was 111 vs 100 predicted.

Method

How it was built

  1. Clean. NOAA GHCN-Daily, station IN012070800. Years kept only with ≥ 85% of monsoon days recorded (63 of 76). The 2005 storm, stored by GHCN as 461 mm on the wrong date, was corrected to IMD's 944 mm.
  2. Describe. Mean, median, mode, variance, skewness, histograms and box plots.
  3. Fit. Normal, Gamma, Gumbel and GEV by maximum likelihood; chi-square and KS goodness of fit; 1,000-sample bootstrap for tail uncertainty.
  4. Test. F, Welch t, Mann–Whitney, ANOVA with Tukey HSD, Poisson z-test, chi-square independence.
  5. Model. Simple and multiple linear regression, with residual, autocorrelation and multicollinearity checks.
  6. Validate. Out-of-sample test on 1991–2024 using models trained on 1950–1990.

This calculator estimates the chance of rainfall at one gauge, not whether a street floods. Flooding also depends on rainfall intensity within the day, tides, drainage and land use. Probabilities assume independent years with constant risk.
